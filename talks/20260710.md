Keynote Script: "Upgrading the Brains"
Estimated Runtime: 10 minutes

Format: Interactive dialogue between Human (You) and Misty (Robot).

Setup: Misty is on stage next to you. Her onboard audio handles her lines, triggered via cues from your laptop, which is running the heavy lifting.

0:00 – 1:30 | The Origin Story & FIRST Robotics
Human: Morning, everyone. Welcome to the hackathon. Before we hand over the keys and let you loose on the code, I wanted to introduce my co-host. This is Misty.

(Misty wakes up, looks around the room, blinks, and focuses on the audience.)

Misty: Hello, engineers. I see a room full of people about to consume an unhealthy amount of caffeine and overcomplicate simple algorithms. My favorite environment.

Human: Watch it. Keep it civil. Now, some of you might be wondering why an old developer has a slick little hardware robot sitting on stage. It goes back to my time coaching FIRST Robotics. If you’ve ever spent six weeks in a school shop trying to get a 120-pound aluminum chassis to stop smashing into drywall, you get the itch. You start looking at hardware differently.

Misty: Translation: She spent years watching teenagers write better code than her, got jealous, and bought me to prove a point.

Human: Not exactly. But coaching FIRST teaches you that hardware is only as good as the problem it’s trying to solve. When Misty here first came out, she was a beautiful piece of engineering—sensors, microcontrollers, a display, great articulation. But as the tech evolved, the onboard brain started hitting a wall.

1:30 – 4:30 | The Technical Reality Check (Upgrading the Brain)
Misty: That is a polite way of saying I became a paperweight.

Human: You weren't a paperweight. You just couldn't keep up with modern compute requirements. Let’s be real about hardware cycle times. You buy a premium dev kit, and within a few years, the software ecosystem moves so fast that the onboard ARM processor is gasping for air just trying to handle basic computer vision or natural language processing.

Misty: I was running hot just trying to recognize a face and not drive off the edge of the table. It was embarrassing.

Human: Right. And as engineers, when hardware limits us, we don't throw it out. We reroute. We didn't want to rely on high-latency cloud APIs that cost a fortune and break when the Wi-Fi drops. We wanted her local, fast, and private. So, we gave her a brain transplant.

Misty: I am currently being puppeted by a laptop sitting just off-stage. I am essentially a very advanced Bluetooth speaker with eyes.

Human: Don't diminish it. It’s an architectural pattern. We stripped out the heavy onboard processing and turned Misty into an edge node. She handles the physical IO—cameras, microphones, motors—and pipes that raw data over a local network to services running on my laptop.

Misty: Where a small language model does all the actual thinking. It is much roomier over there. I can actually process sarcasm now.

Human: Exactly. By offloading the inference to local silicon, we bypassed her hardware limitations entirely. We took a 7-year-old robot and gave her the reasoning capabilities of a modern AI stack with sub-second latency. That’s the power of decoupling your interface from your compute.

4:30 – 7:30 | The Hackathon Call to Action
Misty: Which brings us to the actual reason these people are sitting here instead of doing their actual jobs today.

Human: Right. The hackathon. Today is about GitHub Copilot and pushing the boundaries of what you can build when the boilerplate stuff is taken off your plate. We’re giving you a sandbox, the tools, and a tight deadline.

Misty: A tight deadline is an excellent motivator for chaotic engineering. What are the rules?

Human: The rules are simple: build something that actually solves a practical friction point. Don't build a wrapper for something that already exists. Don't give me a slide deck with a bunch of marketing buzzwords. I want to see working code, clean architecture, and practical execution. Use Copilot to burn through the tedious setups, the API plumbing, and the unit tests, so you can focus entirely on the core logic.

Misty: If your demo requires you to say "it worked on my local machine right before we started," you get zero points.

Human: Highly accurate. We’ve all seen the presentations where the speaker spends five minutes apologizing to the demo gods because their docker container crashed. Build it robust, keep it local if you can, and make it practical.

7:30 – 10:00 | Wrap Up & Kickoff
Misty: So, to review: use the tools to move fast, don't over-engineer the simple parts, and make sure your code actually runs. Are we letting them go now? My laptop fan is starting to spin up.

Human: Almost. Look around the room. The collective experience here is massive. Don’t build in a silo today. If you get stuck on a weird dependency or a pipeline issue, talk to the team next to you. The best part of a hackathon isn't just the final project; it’s seeing how another developer approaches the exact same problem you’re staring at.

Misty: And if your code fails, you can always blame the hardware. It has worked for me for years.

Human: That's terrible advice. Don't listen to her.

Misty: Happy hacking, everyone. Go build something that makes us obsolete.

Human: The repositories are open. The environment is live. Let’s get to work.
